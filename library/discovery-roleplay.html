Scenario

Discovery Roleplay

A stakeholder meeting in a chat window, with no AI anywhere in it. Sam is a scripted persona who answers typed questions by keyword matching — and the scenario is a classic: a manager demanding training for what turns out to be a workflow problem. A coaching panel tracks his mood, the meeting clock, and the clues you uncover, then a scorecard grades the discovery, not the typing.

Live demo — the actual block file. Try asking what changed two weeks ago. Try demanding the training immediately and watch the mood meter.

Add it to Rise

  1. Copy the code below (or download the file).
  2. In your Rise 360 course, insert a custom code block and paste the entire file in — or upload it as a zip package if your version of Rise asks for one.
  3. Preview the lesson. No API keys, no external services — Sam runs entirely in the learner's browser.

Make it yours

  • The whole persona is the TOPICS table at the top of the script: trigger words Sam listens for, his replies as you dig deeper (layers), an optional clue for the case notes, and a mood effect.
  • Write triggers the way people actually talk, including the blunt versions — learners won't phrase questions the way you would.
  • TURNS_TOTAL sets the meeting length; RECOMMENDATIONS holds the closing choices and their coaching text.
  • The sample scenario (a CRM change masquerading as a skills gap) swaps cleanly for any “the request isn't the problem” story in your domain.

Teaching note

When to use it: before teaching needs analysis or stakeholder discovery — or any module whose real lesson is “the first request is rarely the real problem.” Learners who jump to prescribing feel Sam cool in real time; learners who ask about timing and change watch the case notes fill.

Running it live: debrief from the scorecard, not from memory. Ask who found the CRM clue and on which question — the room learns as much from each other's phrasing as from the scenario. The strongest debrief line in the scenario is Sam's own: he wanted training because it was the only fix he could picture.

Honesty note: the block tells learners up front that Sam is scripted. That isn't a weakness — it's the same transparency you'd want from a real AI roleplay, and it sets expectations so a keyword miss reads as a mechanic, not a malfunction.
